Rules of the look

What makes it read as this game

  • Paper, not models. Every character and prop is a flat cutout with a pale cut margin and a little dust in the grain. Only the stage underneath is 3D: the street, the steps, the floor.
  • Ink first. A double-pass scratchy outline on everything, hatching for shade, and almost no smooth gradients on the figures.
  • Puppets on pins. Limbs are separate pieces pinned at the joint and animated by rotation. Landings squash, jumps stretch, hats leave the head.
  • Turning is a flip. A figure turns by spinning edge-on, and the back view is its own drawing swapped in at the thin moment.
  • Depth by layers and fog. Far things go flat and lose their ink. The camera drifts so the layers slide past one another.
  • Light is the only glow. Gas lamps, a lit doorway, a glint on the spectacles. Everything else is matte.
  • Strange, but true. The oddness comes from real history, never invention: a stuffed bear in a window, clothes moths loose in the collection, a stopped clock, a plaster cast of an ancient statue that lost its head long ago, an owl of Athena (Athene noctua), the U carved as V, and Horace’s motto sapere aude: dare to know.
  • One period. Gas street lamps and the first motor cabs share the street, so the stage is set around the 1910s. Props, lettering and trades (bookbinder, taxidermist) stay inside that window.
Cast card

The Archivist

Tall and lanky, coat to the ankles, index cards in every pocket and a quill in the hat. Stands upright with a scholar’s slight forward tilt. Never without the suitcase.

Rig: two legs, two arms, coat, head, hat and scarf. Three head drawings (side, front, back) and two body drawings (side, back).
